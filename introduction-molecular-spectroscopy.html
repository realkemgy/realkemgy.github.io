Introduction to Molecular Spectroscopy

Understanding how electromagnetic radiation interacts with molecules, how transitions between molecular energy levels produce spectra, and how spectroscopic observations reveal molecular properties.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • define spectroscopy and molecular spectroscopy;
  • describe the principal properties of electromagnetic radiation;
  • relate wavelength, frequency, wavenumber and photon energy;
  • explain why molecular energy levels are quantised;
  • describe absorption, emission and scattering processes;
  • explain the origin and basic features of a molecular spectrum;
  • distinguish gross selection rules from specific selection rules; and
  • identify important chemical applications of spectroscopy.

1. Meaning and scope of spectroscopy

Spectroscopy is the study of the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter. During this interaction, matter may absorb, emit or scatter radiation. The measured response can be represented as a spectrum and interpreted to obtain information about the system being studied.

Molecular spectroscopy uses these interactions to probe the energy states and properties of molecules. It provides a way to investigate molecular structure and bonding by examining the frequencies and intensities associated with transitions between molecular states.

Key distinction: spectroscopy is the general study of radiation–matter interactions; molecular spectroscopy focuses on the information obtained from molecules and their quantised states.

The course materials introduce spectroscopy as a tool for determining molecular structure and bonding, identifying unknown compounds, quantifying the amount of a substance, and evaluating properties such as dipole moment and polarizability. These uses arise because different molecular properties influence how radiation is absorbed, emitted or scattered.

What information can a spectrum provide?

  • Evidence about molecular structure and bonding.
  • Information about rotational, vibrational and electronic energy differences.
  • Evidence of molecular symmetry and the activity of particular transitions.
  • Qualitative identification and quantitative measurement of chemical substances.
  • Information about magnetic environments and spin states in magnetic resonance methods.

2. Electromagnetic radiation

Electromagnetic radiation is energy that propagates through space as coupled electric and magnetic fields. The fields oscillate in phase, are perpendicular to each other, and are perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Unlike mechanical waves, electromagnetic radiation does not require a material medium to travel through a vacuum.

2.1 Wave properties

The main quantities used to describe electromagnetic radiation in spectroscopy are wavelength, frequency, period, amplitude, intensity and wavenumber.

QuantitySymbolMeaningCommon unit
WavelengthλDistance between equivalent points on successive cycles, such as crest to crest.m, nm, μm
FrequencyνNumber of complete oscillations passing a point per second.Hz or s⁻¹
PeriodTTime taken for one complete cycle.s
Angular frequencyωFrequency expressed in radians per second.rad s⁻¹
Wavenumberν̃Number of wave cycles per unit distance; commonly the reciprocal of wavelength.cm⁻¹
IntensityIMeasure of radiant power distributed over an area (measurement convention matters).W m⁻²

For a wave travelling in a vacuum, wavelength and frequency are related by the speed of light:

c = λν    and    ω = 2πν

where c is the speed of light in vacuum (approximately 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹). Wavelength and frequency are inversely related: at a fixed wave speed, a shorter wavelength corresponds to a higher frequency.

2.2 Particle description: photons

Electromagnetic radiation also has a particle description. It can be treated as packets of energy called photons. The energy of one photon is proportional to its frequency:

E = hν = hc / λ

Here, h is Planck’s constant. Consequently, high-frequency, short-wavelength radiation has higher photon energy than low-frequency, long-wavelength radiation.

2.3 Wavenumber

Wavenumber is especially useful in infrared and Raman spectroscopy. It is the reciprocal of wavelength when wavelength is expressed in centimetres:

ν̃ = 1 / λ    (λ in cm)

Its common unit is cm⁻¹. Wavenumber is proportional to frequency and photon energy, so a larger wavenumber corresponds to a higher-energy photon.

Worked example 1: Photon energy

Calculate the energy of a photon with wavelength 500 nm.

Convert wavelength: λ = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.

Using E = hc/λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹:

E = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 2.998 × 10⁸) / (5.00 × 10⁻⁷)

E = 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.

3. The electromagnetic spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum covers a continuous range of wavelengths and frequencies. Spectroscopic techniques select particular regions because the photon energies in those regions can couple to different molecular energy changes.

InfraredVibrational transitions involving a change in dipole moment.
Visible and ultravioletElectronic transitions between molecular electronic states.
RadiofrequencyNuclear spin transitions in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), in the presence of a magnetic field.
Microwave / radiofrequency magnetic resonanceElectron spin transitions in electron spin resonance (ESR/EPR), depending on the magnetic field and experimental conditions.
Visible / near-infrared laser excitationRaman spectroscopy measures inelastically scattered light; the Raman shift reports molecular energy changes.

The table is a conceptual guide, not a set of strict non-overlapping boundaries. The exact frequency ranges and experimental arrangements depend on the technique.

4. Molecular energy levels

Molecules possess quantised energy states. Their energy is associated with several types of molecular motion and internal degrees of freedom. The principal contributions introduced in the course materials are electronic, vibrational and rotational energy, together with nuclear and electron spin contributions for magnetic resonance spectroscopy.

Etotal ≈ Eelectronic + Evibrational + Erotational + Espin

This expression is a useful way to organise the contributions to molecular energy. It should not be taken to mean that all contributions are completely independent under every circumstance. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation provides a framework for separating electronic and nuclear motion approximately; interactions and coupling between motions can still influence spectra.

4.1 Electronic energy

Electronic energy is associated with the distribution and motion of electrons in a molecule. Electronic transitions are commonly probed by ultraviolet and visible radiation.

4.2 Vibrational energy

Vibrational energy is associated with periodic motion of atoms about their equilibrium positions. Infrared and Raman spectroscopy commonly probe changes between vibrational states.

4.3 Rotational energy

Rotational energy is associated with the rotation of a molecule as a whole. Rotational transitions of suitable molecules are commonly investigated using microwave spectroscopy.

4.4 Spin energy

Electrons and certain nuclei possess spin and associated magnetic moments. In an external magnetic field, spin energy levels can split, and transitions between them form the basis of ESR/EPR and NMR spectroscopy.

Energy-scale idea: rotational energy spacings are generally smaller than vibrational spacings, which are generally smaller than electronic spacings. This is why different regions of electromagnetic radiation are used to probe different kinds of molecular transitions.

5. How radiation interacts with molecules

When radiation interacts with a molecule, energy may be transferred to or from the molecule, or the radiation may be redirected. Three central processes in optical spectroscopy are absorption, spontaneous emission and stimulated emission. Scattering is also important, especially in Raman spectroscopy.

5.1 Absorption

During absorption, a molecule in a lower energy state takes up a photon and moves to a higher energy state. The photon energy must match the energy difference between the two states:

ΔE = Ef − Ei = hν

If the radiation does not have the required energy, that particular transition will not be induced by one-photon absorption.

5.2 Emission

Emission occurs when an excited molecule moves to a lower energy state and releases energy as a photon. In spontaneous emission, the transition occurs without an incoming photon triggering it. In stimulated emission, an incoming photon induces the excited system to emit another photon with matching energy, phase and direction.

5.3 Scattering

Scattering is the redirection of radiation following its interaction with matter. In elastic scattering, the scattered radiation has the same frequency as the incident radiation. In inelastic scattering, its frequency changes because energy is exchanged with the molecule. Raman scattering is an important example of inelastic scattering.

AbsorptionMoves to a higher energy state.Photon is absorbed.

6. What is a spectrum?

A spectrum is a representation of the measured response of matter as a function of a radiation variable, commonly wavelength, frequency or wavenumber. Depending on the experiment, the response may be recorded as absorbance, transmittance, emitted intensity or scattered intensity.

Spectral features have positions and intensities. The position of a line or band is related to an energy difference between states. Its intensity depends on factors such as the probability of the transition and the number of molecules occupying the initial state. In absorption measurements, concentration and path length also affect the observed signal.

6.1 Absorbance and transmittance

Transmittance is the ratio of transmitted radiant intensity to incident radiant intensity:

T = I / I0     %T = 100T

Absorbance is defined as:

A = −log10(T) = log10(I0/I)

For an absorbing solution under the conditions where the Beer–Lambert relationship applies:

A = εlc

where ε is the molar absorptivity, l is the optical path length and c is the concentration. This relationship provides the basis for quantitative absorption measurements.

Worked example 2: Absorbance from transmittance

A sample has a transmittance of 0.20. Calculate its absorbance.

A = −log10(0.20) = 0.699

Absorbance = 0.699 (dimensionless).

6.2 Spectral line broadening

Idealised models may represent transitions as sharp lines. Real spectra often show lines or bands with finite width. The width can be influenced by the lifetime of excited states, molecular motion (Doppler broadening), collisions and the local environment. A common measure of linewidth is the full width at half maximum (FWHM).

7. Selection rules

A selection rule is a quantum-mechanical condition that determines whether a transition between specified states is allowed or forbidden within a particular approximation. Selection rules arise from the way the radiation field couples to molecular properties, such as electric dipole moment, magnetic dipole moment or polarizability.

For an electric-dipole transition, the transition dipole moment is expressed as an integral involving the initial and final wavefunctions and the dipole moment operator. If the relevant transition moment is zero, the transition is electric-dipole forbidden in that approximation; if it is non-zero, the transition is allowed. An allowed transition is not automatically intense: intensity also depends on the magnitude of the transition moment and other experimental and population factors.

7.1 Gross and specific selection rules

The course text distinguishes two levels of selection rule:

  • Gross (general) selection rule: specifies the molecular property required for a type of spectroscopic activity.
  • Specific selection rule: specifies the permitted changes in quantum numbers for a transition.
TechniqueGeneral requirement highlighted in the course material
Microwave rotational spectroscopyMolecule has a permanent dipole moment.
Infrared vibrational spectroscopyThe vibration produces a change in dipole moment.
Raman spectroscopyThe vibration or rotation produces a change in polarizability.
NMR spectroscopyNuclei with suitable non-zero nuclear spin (magnetically active nuclei).
ESR/EPR spectroscopyUnpaired electrons.

“Forbidden” does not always mean that a transition can never be observed. Some transitions that are forbidden in a simple approximation may occur weakly through higher-order interactions, coupling or relaxation of the assumptions used in deriving the rule.

8. Applications of molecular spectroscopy

  • Structural identification: using characteristic spectral features to help identify functional groups, bonding environments and molecular structure.
  • Quantitative analysis: measuring concentration from an absorption signal, for example using the Beer–Lambert law.
  • Investigating molecular properties: estimating or examining properties such as dipole moment and polarizability.
  • Studying molecular motion: probing rotational and vibrational energy changes.
  • Electronic structure: examining electronic transitions and the energy levels associated with them.
  • Magnetic environments: using NMR and EPR/ESR to investigate nuclear and electron spin environments.

Module summary

  • Spectroscopy studies the interaction of radiation with matter; molecular spectroscopy uses these interactions to probe molecular states and properties.
  • Electromagnetic radiation is described by wavelength, frequency and wavenumber, with photon energy given by E = hν.
  • Molecular energy includes electronic, vibrational, rotational and spin contributions.
  • Absorption, emission and scattering are central radiation–matter processes.
  • A spectrum records a response against wavelength, frequency or wavenumber; spectral positions and intensities carry different kinds of information.
  • Selection rules determine which transitions are allowed within a stated approximation.
  • Different spectroscopic techniques provide complementary information about molecules.
