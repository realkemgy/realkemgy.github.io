General Principles of Molecular Spectroscopy

This chapter develops the physical principles that underlie molecular spectroscopy: radiation, molecular energy states, radiation–matter interactions, transition probabilities, populations, spectral intensities and the factors that determine the appearance of spectral lines.

1. General Introduction to Spectroscopy and Basic Principle

1.1 Meaning and scope of spectroscopy

Spectroscopy is the study of the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter and the analysis of the radiation after that interaction. Depending on the system and experimental conditions, matter may absorb radiation, emit radiation, or scatter it. The measured response is represented as a spectrum, commonly plotted against wavelength, frequency or wavenumber.

Molecular spectroscopy applies these principles to molecules. Molecular structure is the spatial arrangement of atoms and the associated distribution of electrons. The structure determines the allowed molecular energy states and influences the frequencies and probabilities of transitions between them. Spectroscopy therefore provides an experimental route to investigate molecular structure, bonding, molecular motion and selected physical properties.

Spectroscopic measurements have qualitative and quantitative uses. They can assist in identifying unknown compounds, examining bonding and functional groups, determining molecular parameters, measuring concentrations, and studying molecular environments and dynamic processes. The information obtained depends on the type of radiation and the molecular property with which it interacts.

1.2 The basic principle

The central principle is that molecular energy is quantised. A molecule cannot absorb or emit an arbitrary amount of energy in a transition between stationary states. For a transition from an initial state i to a final state f, the energy difference is

ΔE = Ef − Ei = hν

where h is Planck's constant and ν is the frequency of the photon. For absorption, Ef is greater than Ei; for emission, the molecule moves from a higher to a lower energy state and the emitted photon carries the energy difference. The radiation frequency must correspond to the separation between the states, subject to the relevant transition probability and selection rules.

Incident radiation→Molecule→Absorption / emission / scattering→Measured spectrum

1.3 Why molecular spectra are informative

Molecules possess electronic, vibrational and rotational states, as well as nuclear and electron spin states. The energy separations are different for these motions. Consequently, different radiation frequencies probe different molecular degrees of freedom. A spectrum records the allowed transitions and their intensities; interpreting these features gives information about the molecule.

Important distinction. A spectral line position primarily reports an energy difference. Its intensity is governed by transition probability and the population of the initial state, together with experimental factors. These are related but distinct pieces of information.

2. The Electromagnetic Radiation

2.1 Nature of electromagnetic radiation

Electromagnetic radiation is energy propagated through space or a material medium as coupled electric and magnetic fields. In a plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum, the electric field E and magnetic field B oscillate in phase, perpendicular to one another and perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Electromagnetic radiation does not require a material medium and travels in vacuum at the speed of light, c.

A simplified linearly polarised plane wave travelling along the z direction may be written as

E(z,t) = E0 cos(kz − ωt)     B(z,t) = B0 cos(kz − ωt)

Here E0 and B0 are field amplitudes, k is the wave number in radians per metre, ω is angular frequency and t is time. In vacuum, E0 = cB0. The phase term indicates the position of the wave at a particular time.

For radiation travelling in vacuum, the fundamental relationship is

c = λν     ;     ω = 2πν     ;     k = 2π/λ

The speed of light is c = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. In a medium, the phase velocity is generally lower and the wavelength changes; the frequency remains fixed when radiation crosses a stationary boundary.

2.3 Polarisation and intensity

Polarisation describes the orientation and trajectory of the electric-field vector. In linear polarisation, the electric field oscillates along a fixed direction. Circular and elliptical polarisation arise when two perpendicular field components have appropriate amplitude and phase relationships. Polarisation is important in spectroscopy because transition probabilities depend on the orientation of the transition moment relative to the radiation field.

For a plane wave, the time-averaged energy flux is described by the Poynting vector. In vacuum, the average intensity is proportional to the square of the electric-field amplitude:

I = (1/2) ε0 c E02

Thus, doubling the field amplitude produces four times the intensity, for otherwise identical conditions. Intensity is not simply the amplitude itself; it is proportional to its square.

2.4 Photon description and energy

Electromagnetic radiation also exhibits particle-like behaviour. Its energy is exchanged in discrete packets called photons. The energy of one photon is

E = hν = hc/λ = hcν̃

where h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. In the final expression, ν̃ must be expressed in reciprocal metres if E is required in joules. If wavenumber is in cm⁻¹, the conversion to m⁻¹ is required.

2.5 Wavelength, frequency and wavenumber conversions

ν̃ = 1/λ    (λ in cm)     ;     ν = cν̃
Worked example 2.1. Radiation has a wavelength of 589 nm. Calculate its frequency, wavenumber and photon energy.

λ = 589 × 10⁻⁹ m. Therefore, ν = c/λ = (2.998 × 10⁸)/(589 × 10⁻⁹) = 5.09 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹.

ν̃ = 1/(5.89 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 1.698 × 10⁶ m⁻¹ = 1.698 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹.

E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)(5.09 × 10¹⁴) = 3.37 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.

3. The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum is the continuous range of electromagnetic radiation arranged according to wavelength, frequency or photon energy. The regions are conventionally named radiofrequency, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma radiation. Their boundaries are approximate rather than absolute.

Different regions interact with different molecular degrees of freedom because the energy of a photon depends on its frequency. A useful broad relationship is that rotational transitions generally require less energy than vibrational transitions, while electronic transitions generally require more energy.

Region (approximate)Typical interaction or spectroscopic useInformation commonly obtained
RadiofrequencyNuclear spin transitions in a magnetic fieldNMR: chemical environments, connectivity and molecular dynamics
MicrowaveRotational transitions; electron spin resonance in suitable conditionsRotational constants, moments of inertia, molecular geometry; EPR/ESR spin information
Far- and mid-infraredRotational-vibrational and vibrational transitionsBond force constants, vibrational frequencies and functional-group information
Near-infraredVibrational overtones and combination bandsComposition and molecular-bond information
Visible and ultravioletElectronic excitationElectronic states, conjugation and chromophore properties
X-rayCore-electron excitation/ionisation and diffraction interactionsElement-specific electronic structure and structural information, depending on method
GammaNuclear transitions and nuclear-resonance phenomenaNuclear energy states and specialised spectroscopic information

Raman spectroscopy is not assigned to a unique incident-light region in the same way as a simple absorption technique. It measures the frequency shift between incident and scattered photons. Visible or near-infrared lasers are commonly used, but the Raman shift corresponds to molecular rotational or vibrational energy differences.

Interpretive principle. The spectrum region identifies the approximate energy scale being probed; the actual spectroscopic activity also depends on molecular properties and transition selection rules.

4. Molecular Energy Levels and Born–Oppenheimer Approximation

4.1 Quantised molecular energy

A molecule is a quantum-mechanical system. Its allowed stationary states are described by solutions of the molecular Schrödinger equation. The total molecular energy contains contributions from electronic motion, nuclear motion (vibration and rotation), translation and spin interactions. A useful approximate decomposition is

E ≈ Eelectronic + Evibrational + Erotational + Etranslational + Espin

The decomposition is a model for organising the energy contributions. Coupling between motions can occur, so the contributions are not always strictly independent.

4.2 Relative energy scales

For many molecules, rotational energy spacings are the smallest, vibrational spacings are larger, and electronic energy differences are much larger. The rough ordering is

ΔErot ≪ ΔEvib ≪ ΔEelec

This ordering explains why microwave or far-infrared radiation is often associated with rotational transitions, infrared radiation with vibrational transitions, and visible/ultraviolet radiation with electronic transitions. It is an energy-scale guide, not a universal numerical boundary.

4.3 Born–Oppenheimer approximation

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation is based on the large difference between electron and nuclear masses. Electrons are much lighter and respond much more rapidly than nuclei. To a first approximation, the nuclei can therefore be treated as fixed while the electronic Schrödinger equation is solved. The resulting electronic energy depends parametrically on the nuclear coordinates and forms a potential-energy surface on which nuclear motion occurs.

The molecular wavefunction is approximately factorised into an electronic part and a nuclear part:

Ψ(r,R) ≈ ψe(r;R) χ(R)

Here r denotes electronic coordinates and R denotes nuclear coordinates. The electronic wavefunction ψe is solved for fixed nuclear geometry R; χ describes nuclear motion on the corresponding electronic potential-energy surface. Further separation of nuclear motion into vibration and rotation is an additional useful approximation, not the Born–Oppenheimer approximation itself.

4.4 Consequences for molecular spectroscopy

  • Electronic states have associated vibrational levels.
  • Vibrational states have associated rotational structure.
  • Electronic, vibrational and rotational contributions can often be analysed in stages because their energy scales differ.
  • Interactions between motions lead to effects such as rovibrational coupling, perturbations and breakdown of simple approximations.
Clarification. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation does not state that electronic, vibrational and rotational motions are absolutely independent. It provides the first major separation of electronic and nuclear motion. Spectroscopic fine structure and coupling effects reveal where additional terms are needed.

5. Molecular Modes of Interactions and Transitions

Radiation interacts with molecules through electric and magnetic fields. The electric field commonly couples to the molecular electric dipole moment; magnetic interactions couple to magnetic moments. The nature of the coupling determines the type and probability of a transition.

5.1 Stimulated absorption

Consider a molecule initially in a lower state 1 with energy E₁. When radiation of frequency ν = (E₂ − E₁)/h is incident, the molecule may absorb a photon and move to state 2. The process is stimulated because the incident radiation induces the transition.

Molecule(1) + hν → Molecule(2)    ;    E₂ − E₁ = hν

Absorption removes energy from the radiation field and increases the molecular energy. In an ensemble, the absorption rate depends on the population of the lower state, the radiation energy density and the transition probability.

5.2 Spontaneous emission

An excited molecule can return to a lower energy state without an external photon triggering the event. The energy difference is released as a photon. For an isolated transition, spontaneous emission occurs with a characteristic probability per unit time described by the Einstein A coefficient.

Molecule(2) → Molecule(1) + hν

Spontaneously emitted photons have random emission times and, for an isotropic ensemble without imposed coherence, are emitted in different directions and phases. The excited-state population decays exponentially when a single first-order radiative channel dominates.

5.3 Stimulated emission

An incident photon whose frequency matches the transition can induce an excited molecule to emit a second photon while it falls to the lower state. The emitted photon is coherent with the stimulating radiation: it has the same frequency, phase, direction and polarisation in the idealised description. Stimulated emission is the fundamental amplification process in a laser.

Molecule(2) + hν → Molecule(1) + 2hν (outgoing radiation)

For net optical amplification, stimulated emission must exceed absorption. This requires population inversion after accounting for the degeneracies of the states.

5.4 Scattering

Scattering occurs when incident radiation interacts with a molecule and is redirected. In elastic (Rayleigh) scattering, the molecule returns to its original energy state and the scattered photon has the same frequency as the incident photon. In inelastic scattering, energy is exchanged with the molecule and the scattered photon frequency changes.

Scattering typeEnergy exchangeScattered photon
Rayleigh (elastic)No net molecular energy changeSame frequency as incident radiation
Stokes RamanMolecule gains energyLower frequency / lower photon energy
Anti-Stokes RamanMolecule loses energyHigher frequency / higher photon energy

Raman scattering is usually much weaker than Rayleigh scattering but provides information about changes in molecular rotational or vibrational energy. Stokes scattering commonly exceeds anti-Stokes scattering in intensity because, at ordinary temperatures, more molecules occupy lower-energy initial states than thermally excited states.

5.5 Magnetic interactions

Some spectroscopic transitions arise from the interaction of magnetic moments with an external magnetic field. A magnetic moment μ in a magnetic field B has an interaction energy described by

E = − μ · B

For a spin-½ particle in a field directed along z, the two spin orientations have different energies. The separation depends on the gyromagnetic ratio and the applied magnetic field. In NMR, the magnetic moments of suitable nuclei interact with the field; in EPR/ESR, unpaired electron spins are probed. The resonance condition is conventionally expressed as

ΔE = hν = ℏω

The resonance frequency depends on the magnetic field and the magnetic properties of the nucleus or electron. Magnetic resonance therefore probes spin states rather than ordinary electric-dipole rotational or vibrational transitions.

Scope of this section. The detailed energy-level treatment, chemical shifts, hyperfine interactions and instrument principles are developed in the dedicated magnetic resonance chapters. Here the essential point is that magnetic fields couple to magnetic moments and can split spin energy levels.

6. Intensities of Spectral Lines

6.1 What determines line intensity?

The position of a spectral line is determined by the energy difference between the states. The intensity depends on how likely the transition is and how many molecules are available in the initial state. For absorption, a strong line generally requires a substantial lower-state population and a large transition probability. For emission, the upper-state population and emission probability are central.

For an electric-dipole transition between states i and f, the transition dipole moment is

μfi = ⟨ψf| μ̂ |ψi⟩ = ∫ ψf* μ̂ ψi dτ

The transition probability is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the transition moment, |μfi|², subject to the radiation field, state populations, degeneracies and line-shape factors. A zero transition moment makes the transition electric-dipole forbidden in that approximation.

6.2 Population and transition probability

For a simple transition between lower state 1 and upper state 2, the net absorption depends on absorption from state 1 minus stimulated emission from state 2. In the absence of degeneracy complications, this is proportional to the population difference N₁ − N₂. With degeneracies, the statistical weights and the appropriate Einstein B coefficients must be included.

6.3 Other factors affecting measured intensity

  • Transition moment: a larger transition moment generally gives a stronger transition.
  • Population of the initial state: only molecules occupying the relevant initial state can undergo that transition.
  • Degeneracy: the number of states at a given energy affects the total population and observed intensity.
  • Radiation intensity: stimulated transition rates depend on the incident radiation energy density.
  • Concentration and path length: these affect absorption measured through a sample.
  • Line broadening and overlap: broadening redistributes intensity over frequency and overlapping lines can complicate measured peak heights.
  • Instrument response: detector sensitivity, optical throughput and spectral resolution influence observed signals.
Peak height versus integrated intensity. Broadening can reduce a line's peak height even when its integrated area is conserved. For quantitative comparisons, distinguish peak height from integrated line intensity.

7. Population of States and Radiative Processes

7.1 Boltzmann distribution

At thermal equilibrium, the population of molecular states follows the Boltzmann distribution. The number of molecules in a state i of energy Eᵢ and degeneracy gᵢ is proportional to its statistical weight and Boltzmann factor:

Nᵢ / N = (gᵢ e−Eᵢ/kT) / Q

where k is Boltzmann's constant, T is absolute temperature and Q is the molecular partition function:

Q = Σᵢ gᵢ e−Eᵢ/kT

The population ratio for two states is

N₂/N₁ = (g₂/g₁) exp[−(E₂ − E₁)/kT]

For equal degeneracies, this reduces to the simple exponential ratio. If energies are expressed per mole, replace k by the gas constant R. Higher-energy states are less populated at thermal equilibrium, although increasing temperature increases the relative population of excited states.

Worked example 7.1. Two non-degenerate states are separated by 11.9 kJ mol⁻¹ at 300 K. Estimate N₂/N₁.

Use N₂/N₁ = exp(−ΔE/RT), R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and ΔE = 11900 J mol⁻¹.

N₂/N₁ = exp[−11900/(8.314 × 300)] = 8.4 × 10⁻³

Thus, the upper state contains about 0.84% as many molecules as the lower state, under the stated assumptions.

7.2 Einstein's coefficients

Einstein described radiative transitions between two energy levels using three coefficients. Let level 1 be the lower state and level 2 the upper state, with E₂ − E₁ = hν. Let ρ(ν) denote the spectral energy density of the radiation field at the transition frequency.

CoefficientProcessRate for the ensemble
B₁₂Stimulated absorption, 1 → 2N₁ B₁₂ ρ(ν)
B₂₁Stimulated emission, 2 → 1N₂ B₂₁ ρ(ν)
A₂₁Spontaneous emission, 2 → 1N₂ A₂₁

The net rate of change in the upper-state population for this two-level model is

dN₂/dt = N₁B₁₂ρ(ν) − N₂B₂₁ρ(ν) − N₂A₂₁

The first term increases the upper-state population; the latter two terms decrease it. At thermal equilibrium, upward and downward rates balance.

7.3 Einstein relations

For coefficients defined using spectral energy density per unit frequency and the usual degeneracy convention, the Einstein relations are

g₁ B₁₂ = g₂ B₂₁
A₂₁ / B₂₁ = 8πhν³ / c³

Here g₁ and g₂ are the degeneracies of the lower and upper levels. If the two levels have equal degeneracy, B₁₂ = B₂₁. The relation between A and B shows that spontaneous emission becomes increasingly important relative to stimulated emission as transition frequency increases, for the same spectral energy density convention.

These relations follow by requiring the two-level system in thermal equilibrium with radiation to reproduce the Planck black-body radiation distribution. They connect microscopic transition probabilities to the macroscopic radiation field.

7.4 Radiation theory and Planck distribution

Black-body radiation is the equilibrium radiation emitted by an ideal absorber at temperature T. The spectral energy density per unit frequency is

ρ(ν) = [8πhν³/c³] / [exp(hν/kT) − 1]

This Planck distribution contains both the density of electromagnetic modes and the mean photon energy in each mode. Substituting it into the Einstein rate equations yields the Einstein coefficient relations. The expression is frequency-based; a spectral density defined per unit wavelength has a different mathematical form and units.

7.5 Spontaneous versus stimulated emission

For the upper state, the ratio of spontaneous to stimulated emission rates is A₂₁/[B₂₁ρ(ν)]. In a thermal radiation field, the Einstein relations give

A₂₁/[B₂₁ρ(ν)] = exp(hν/kT) − 1

When hν is much smaller than kT, stimulated emission can dominate in a thermal radiation field. At high photon energies relative to thermal energy, spontaneous emission is comparatively more important. The relative importance in an actual experiment also depends on the radiation field present.

7.6 Population inversion

At thermal equilibrium, the lower state is more populated than the upper state after accounting for degeneracy. Laser amplification requires a non-equilibrium population distribution in which the upper state is sufficiently populated relative to the lower state. This is called population inversion. In the simplest equal-degeneracy two-level description, inversion means N₂ > N₁; more generally the comparison must account for g₁ and g₂.

8. Concentration, Path Length and the Beer–Lambert Law

8.1 Transmittance and absorbance

When a beam of monochromatic radiation passes through an absorbing sample, its intensity decreases. Let I₀ be the incident intensity and I the transmitted intensity. The transmittance T is

T = I/I₀     ;     %T = 100T

Absorbance is defined using the base-10 logarithm:

A = −log₁₀T = log₁₀(I₀/I)

Absorbance is dimensionless. A larger absorbance corresponds to a smaller transmittance.

8.2 Derivation of the Beer–Lambert relationship

Consider a thin layer of solution of thickness dl containing an absorbing species at concentration c. For monochromatic radiation and a homogeneous sample, the decrease in intensity is proportional to the incident intensity, concentration and layer thickness:

dI = −κcI dl

Rearranging and integrating from I₀ at l = 0 to I at path length l gives

∫(dI/I) = −κc∫dl    ⇒    ln(I/I₀) = −κcl

Therefore,

I = I₀e−κcl

Converting to base-10 absorbance gives

A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = (κ/2.303)cl = εcl

where ε = κ/2.303 is the molar absorptivity when concentration is expressed in mol L⁻¹ and path length in cm. In those common units, ε is reported in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹.

8.4 Worked applications

Worked example 8.1. A solution has ε = 3200 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, c = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ and l = 1.00 cm. Calculate absorbance and percentage transmittance.

A = εlc = 3200 × 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.00 = 1.088.

T = 10⁻ᴬ = 10⁻¹·⁰⁸⁸ = 0.0817.

%T = 100 × 0.0817 = 8.17%.

Worked example 8.2. A sample transmits 19.2% of incident radiation. Calculate its absorbance.

T = 19.2/100 = 0.192.

A = −log₁₀(0.192) = 0.717

8.5 Conditions and limitations

The Beer–Lambert law is most reliable for dilute, homogeneous solutions, with radiation of sufficiently narrow bandwidth and a chemically stable absorbing species. Deviations may arise from high concentration, solute–solute or solute–solvent interactions, changes in chemical equilibria, polychromatic radiation, stray light, scattering, fluorescence, instrumental effects or departures from ideal solution behaviour. The absorptivity is wavelength-dependent; it is not a universal constant for a substance at all wavelengths.

9. Selection Rules

9.1 Meaning of a selection rule

A selection rule is a quantum-mechanical condition that determines whether a transition between specified states has a non-zero probability for a particular interaction. The rule follows from the symmetry and mathematical form of the interaction operator and the initial and final wavefunctions.

For an electric-dipole transition, the transition moment is

μfi = ∫ ψf* μ̂ ψi dτ

If the integral is zero, the transition is electric-dipole forbidden within that approximation. If it is non-zero, the transition is allowed. The magnitude of the integral influences transition strength; allowed does not mean equally intense.

9.2 Gross and specific selection rules

Gross (general) selection rules identify the molecular property required for activity in a particular spectroscopic method. Specific selection rules describe the allowed changes in quantum numbers for a transition within a model of the energy levels.

TechniqueGross activity conditionIllustrative specific rule / comment
Pure rotational electric-dipolePermanent molecular dipole momentFor a diatomic rigid rotor, ΔJ = ±1
Infrared vibrationVibration must change the dipole momentHarmonic oscillator: Δv = ±1; anharmonicity permits weak overtones
RamanMotion must change molecular polarizabilityFor a linear rigid rotor, ΔJ = 0, ±2; vibrational Raman activity requires a polarizability change
Electronic electric-dipoleNon-zero electronic transition dipole integralAtomic-like rules include Δl = ±1 and spin conservation in the electric-dipole approximation; molecular symmetry and vibronic effects also matter
NMRNucleus has non-zero nuclear spin and magnetic momentFor a simple spin transition, ΔmI = ±1
EPR/ESRUnpaired electron with magnetic momentFor a simple spin transition, ΔmS = ±1

9.3 Why forbidden transitions may still be observed

Forbidden generally means forbidden under a stated approximation, not impossible under all circumstances. Weak transitions can gain intensity through vibronic coupling, spin–orbit coupling, magnetic-dipole or higher multipole interactions, symmetry-breaking distortions, or perturbations that mix states of different character. The intensity is usually lower than that of a strongly allowed transition.

Interpretation. Selection rules explain why some expected transitions are absent or weak. They must be applied to the relevant spectroscopic interaction; an IR selection rule is not interchangeable with a Raman or magnetic-resonance selection rule.

10. Spectral Line Broadening

10.1 Linewidth and line shape

An ideal transition between infinitely long-lived stationary states would produce a sharply defined frequency. Real transitions have finite linewidths. A linewidth is commonly specified as the full width at half maximum (FWHM), the frequency interval between the two points where the spectral intensity falls to half its maximum value. The observed line shape may be Lorentzian, Gaussian or a convolution of both (Voigt).

10.2 Natural (lifetime) broadening

Natural broadening arises because excited states have finite lifetimes. The energy–time uncertainty relation is commonly written as

ΔE Δt ≳ ℏ/2

A short-lived state has a larger energy uncertainty than a long-lived state. This produces a finite spread in transition frequency. For a simple transition with a stable lower state and an upper-state population lifetime τ, the natural Lorentzian FWHM is

Δνnatural = 1/(2πτ)

The exact linewidth depends on the lifetimes of both states and on the definition of coherence time. Natural broadening is homogeneous: the same intrinsic mechanism acts on each member of an identical ensemble.

10.3 Doppler broadening

Doppler broadening arises from the thermal motion of absorbing or emitting molecules. Molecules moving towards or away from the radiation source experience slightly shifted frequencies due to the Doppler effect. An ensemble contains a distribution of velocities, producing a distribution of observed transition frequencies. The resulting profile is Gaussian and is inhomogeneous.

For a gas of particles of mass m at temperature T, the Doppler FWHM in frequency is

ΔνD = (ν₀/c) √[8kT ln 2 / m]

where ν₀ is the unshifted transition frequency. Doppler width increases with frequency and with the square root of temperature, and decreases with the square root of molecular mass. Cooling the gas or using heavier species reduces Doppler broadening.

10.4 Collisional (pressure) broadening

Collisions between molecules perturb energy levels and interrupt the phase evolution of the radiating dipole. These interruptions shorten the effective coherence time and broaden the spectral line. In gases, collision frequency generally increases with pressure, so collisional linewidth often increases approximately with pressure over a suitable range. Collisional broadening is homogeneous and is commonly associated with a Lorentzian profile.

10.5 Other sources of broadening

  • Instrumental broadening: finite spectrometer resolution, slit width, optical imperfections and detector response broaden the measured feature. It is an instrumental contribution rather than an intrinsic molecular linewidth.
  • Stark broadening: external electric fields perturb energy levels and transition frequencies.
  • Zeeman broadening or splitting: external magnetic fields alter magnetic-sublevel energies; depending on resolution and conditions, this may appear as splitting or broadening.
  • Van der Waals / environmental broadening: intermolecular interactions perturb transition energies and lifetimes.
  • Resonance broadening: resonant interactions or energy exchange between identical or similar species can broaden a transition under appropriate conditions.
  • Inhomogeneous sample effects: distributions of local environments, conformations, fields or chemical surroundings produce a spread of transition frequencies.

10.6 Homogeneous and inhomogeneous broadening; combined profiles

TypePhysical originTypical line shape
Natural lifetimeFinite state lifetimeLorentzian
Collisional / pressureCollision-induced phase interruption and level perturbationLorentzian (often)
DopplerThermal velocity distributionGaussian
InstrumentalFinite instrument resolutionDepends on instrument function

When Gaussian and Lorentzian mechanisms are both important, the observed profile is often described by a Voigt function, the convolution of the two. The measured width is not generally obtained by simply adding all individual FWHM values; the combination depends on the line-shape model.

Practical significance. Broadening determines how well neighbouring transitions can be resolved. Lower pressure can reduce collisional broadening; lower temperature can reduce Doppler broadening; longer-lived states have narrower natural linewidths; and higher instrumental resolution reduces instrumental broadening.
